In the near future, Earth is slowly dying. Dust storms swallow the crops, and humanity has gone back to being, above all, farmers. Against this backdrop Cooper (Matthew McConaughey), a former NASA pilot, accepts a mission through a wormhole near Saturn in search of a new home, leaving behind his children, among them young Murph.

## Science as the engine of emotion

Physicist Kip Thorne consulted on the film, and it shows on screen: the image of Gargantua, the black hole, was born from real equations. But Nolan never uses science as decoration. Time dilation, explained in one simple scene, becomes the film's most concrete source of pain: every hour on a planet near the black hole costs years on Earth, and Cooper watches his children grow old in recorded messages.

## Sound, silence and an organ

Hans Zimmer's score, built around a church organ, gives space an almost religious weight. And Nolan knows when to cut everything out: the vacuum here is silent, and that silence is scarier than any explosion.

## Is it worth it?

Interstellar has expository dialogue and a third act that splits opinions. Even so, few blockbusters risk this much on such an intimate idea: love as something that travels through time. It runs nearly three hours, and they fly by.
